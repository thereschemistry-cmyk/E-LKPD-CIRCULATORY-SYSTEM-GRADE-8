🗓️ Meeting 1 · Heart Structure & Double Circulation

AO1 · Explaining Phenomena Scientifically. Study the heart diagrams, then apply your understanding to real-life scenarios.

🗓️ MEETING 1 of 4
🫀 Heart Structure & Double Circulation
📘 Content FocusHeart structure & double circulation, and its advantages
📊 PISA CompetencyExplaining phenomena scientifically (EPS)
🌍 Cambridge Life SkillsPractical problem-solving — analysing the blood circulation pathway
🎯 Related AOAO1
The first meeting lays the conceptual foundation for the whole unit. Students learn the four chambers of the heart (left/right atrium, left/right ventricle), the heart valves, and how blood flows through two separate circuits — the pulmonary circuit (heart ↔ lungs) and the systemic circuit (heart ↔ rest of the body). Because humans have a double circulation, blood passes through the heart twice in one full loop, which keeps oxygenated and deoxygenated blood from mixing and allows blood pressure to the body to be kept higher and more efficient than in a single circulation. Understanding this advantage is a classic example of the Explaining Phenomena Scientifically competency: students are trained to explain "why" a biological structure works the way it does, rather than simply memorising its labels.
  • Learning activities: studying a labelled heart diagram, tracing the double circulation pathway interactively, and comparing the efficiency of double vs single circulation.
  • Life Skills scenario: students practise practical problem-solving by working through real cases (e.g. why a marathon runner breathes faster, why trekkers feel breathless at high altitude) using their understanding of the circulation pathway.
  • Outcome (AO1): students can explain the structure of the heart and the mechanism of double circulation, together with its advantages, in a scientific and logically ordered way.
🔊
🎧 Audio – Meeting 1 Overview
Listen while you study the diagrams
The human heart is a muscular pump located slightly left of centre in the chest. It has four chambers: the right atrium, right ventricle, left atrium, and left ventricle. Blood travels through two main circuits: the pulmonary circuit to the lungs, and the systemic circuit to the rest of the body. This double circulation keeps oxygenated and deoxygenated blood separate, and helps maintain higher blood pressure to the body.

🗓️ Meeting 2 · Blood Vessels & Blood Components

AO2 · Interpreting Data & Evidence. Compare blood vessel types, examine a blood smear, then interpret blood test data.

🗓️ MEETING 2 of 4
🩸 Blood Vessels & Blood Components
📘 Content FocusBlood vessels (arteries/veins/capillaries) & blood components, including haemoglobin & anaemia
📊 PISA CompetencyInterpreting data and scientific evidence (IDE)
🌍 Cambridge Life SkillsHandling data — interpreting a simple CBC (Complete Blood Count) result
🎯 Related AOAO2
The second meeting shifts from heart structure to the network of blood vessels that carries blood around the body. Students compare three vessel types — arteries (thick, elastic walls, carrying high-pressure blood away from the heart), veins (valved, carrying low-pressure blood back to the heart), and capillaries (one cell thick, the site of exchange) — before moving on to the components of blood: plasma, red blood cells (erythrocytes) with their haemoglobin, white blood cells, and platelets. Haemoglobin becomes the bridge to the concept of anaemia, a condition of low haemoglobin level that reduces the blood's capacity to carry oxygen. Because this meeting focuses on the Interpreting Data & Evidence competency, students not only learn the concepts but also practise reading and interpreting simple laboratory data, similar to a Complete Blood Count (CBC) result.
  • Learning activities: examining blood vessel structure, a simple practical observing a blood smear, and comparing blood composition data for a healthy person vs. a patient with anaemia.
  • Life Skills scenario: handling data — students practise reading normal vs. abnormal values on a simple CBC result and drawing conclusions about health status from that data.
  • Outcome (AO2): students can interpret data and scientific evidence relating to blood components and connect it to conditions such as anaemia.
🔊
🎧 Audio – Meeting 2 Overview
Listen while you study the vessels and blood components
Blood vessels form a network of arteries, veins, and capillaries. Arteries carry blood away from the heart under high pressure. Veins carry blood back to the heart and contain valves to prevent backflow. Capillaries are just one cell thick, allowing exchange of gases and nutrients. Blood itself is made of plasma, red blood cells containing haemoglobin, white blood cells, and platelets. Haemoglobin carries oxygen around the body; when its level is too low, a person may develop anaemia.

🧫 Hands-on practical: see these components for yourself
🎯 Objectives & Safety

By the end of this practical, you should be able to prepare a thin blood smear, stain it correctly, and identify red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets under the microscope.

⚠️
Safety first: Blood carries infection risk. Schools should use ready-made, commercially prepared blood smear slides wherever possible. If a fresh sample must be taken, it should only be done under direct teacher supervision with a sterile, single-use lancet and disposable gloves, with sharps disposed of immediately in a biohazard container. Never share a lancet between students, and any student may choose to observe a partner's slide instead of providing a sample.
🧪 Materials
  • Compound light microscope, with a 100× oil-immersion objective
  • Clean glass microscope slides and a "spreader" slide
  • Blood sample (or a ready-made, pre-stained slide)
  • Giemsa or Wright's stain, and methanol fixative
  • Immersion oil, lens tissue, disposable gloves
📋 Procedure
  1. Place one small drop of blood near one end of a clean slide.
  2. Hold a second "spreader" slide at a 30–45° angle in front of the drop. Let the blood spread along its edge, then push it smoothly forward in one continuous motion to create a thin film.
  3. Air-dry the smear completely, then fix it with a few drops of methanol for about 1 minute.
  4. Flood the slide with Giemsa or Wright's stain and leave for the recommended time (check the stain's instructions — typically 10–20 minutes).
  5. Rinse gently with buffered water, then stand the slide upright to air-dry.
  6. Examine under low power first to find a well-spread area, then switch to the oil-immersion objective (100×) for a detailed view.

AO3 · Evaluating & Designing Scientific Enquiry. Design and evaluate investigations, then interpret real exercise data.

🗓️ MEETING 3 of 4
❤️ Pulse Rate, Blood Pressure & Cardiac Output
📘 Content FocusMeasuring pulse rate & blood pressure, effects of exercise, cardiac output
📊 PISA CompetencyEvaluating & designing scientific enquiry (EDSE)
🌍 Cambridge Life SkillsApplying scientific method — designing an investigation into the effect of physical activity
🎯 Related AOAO3
The third meeting is more practical and investigative. Students learn to measure pulse rate at the radial/carotid point and understand the two numbers in a blood pressure reading (systolic/diastolic), then calculate cardiac output using the formula Cardiac Output = Heart Rate × Stroke Volume. The main focus is on how these variables change during physical activity, and why those changes make physiological sense (muscles need more oxygen when moving). Because the targeted competency is Evaluating & Designing Scientific Enquiry, the emphasis is not just on taking measurements but on designing a fair test: identifying the independent, dependent, and controlled variables, choosing an appropriate sample size, and evaluating the weaknesses of an experimental design (e.g. a school fitness study, the effect of caffeine on heart rate, or the effect of body position).
  • Learning activities: practising measuring their own pulse rate, analysing case studies (School Fitness Study, Caffeine and Heart Rate, Body Position), and calculating cardiac output from given data.
  • Life Skills scenario: applying scientific method — students design the steps of an investigation to test the effect of physical activity on heart rate, including how variables are controlled.
  • Outcome (AO3): students can evaluate and design a simple scientific investigation relating to pulse rate, blood pressure, and the effects of exercise.
🔊
🎧 Audio – Meeting 3 Overview
Listen to the key ideas before you begin
Pulse rate can be measured at the radial or carotid artery and reflects how many times the heart beats per minute. Blood pressure is recorded as two numbers: systolic pressure, when the heart contracts, and diastolic pressure, when it relaxes. Cardiac output is the volume of blood the heart pumps per minute, calculated as heart rate multiplied by stroke volume. During exercise, heart rate, blood pressure, and cardiac output all increase to meet the muscles' greater demand for oxygen.

The final meeting is the capstone of the unit: students bring together their understanding of structure (Meeting 1), blood components (Meeting 2), and investigation/data skills (Meeting 3) to examine a real health issue. Atherosclerosis — the build-up of fatty plaques that harden and narrow arteries — is discussed as an example of how lifestyle (a diet high in saturated fat, insufficient exercise, stress, smoking) affects the blood vessel structure studied earlier, and can lead to a heart attack or stroke. Students are also introduced to the basic principles of CPR (Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation) as an emergency response when the heart stops pumping effectively, linking the circulation concepts from Meeting 1 to a life-saving action. Because this is a synthesis meeting, students are asked to read epidemiological data (for example, the prevalence of cardiovascular disease linked to lifestyle habits) and use that evidence to make an evidence-based decision — a core Life Skills competency for handling a socio-scientific issue such as a public health campaign.
  • Learning activities: discussing cardiovascular risk factor case studies, analysing simple epidemiological data, and building an evidence-based argument for a health campaign.
  • Life Skills scenario: students take on the role of a decision-maker (e.g. a school or family policy planner) who must weigh scientific evidence before recommending preventive action.
  • Outcome (AO1+AO2): students can scientifically explain the phenomenon of cardiovascular disorders (AO1) while also interpreting epidemiological data/evidence to support an evidence-based decision (AO2).

📖 Key Vocabulary Glossary

Review important terms for the Human Circulatory System.

🔊
🎧 Audio – Glossary Reading
Listen to key terms
Heart. The muscular organ that pumps blood around the body. Atrium. Upper chamber of the heart that receives blood. Ventricle. Lower chamber of the heart that pumps blood out. Artery. Blood vessel that carries blood away from the heart. Vein. Blood vessel that carries blood towards the heart. Capillary. The smallest blood vessel where exchange of substances occurs. Haemoglobin. Iron-containing protein in red blood cells that binds oxygen. Plasma. The liquid component of blood. Cardiac output. The volume of blood pumped by the heart per minute. Pulmonary circulation. The circuit of blood between the heart and the lungs. Systemic circulation. The circuit of blood between the heart and the rest of the body. Hypertension. Persistently high blood pressure, a risk factor for cardiovascular disease. Atherosclerosis. The build-up of fatty plaques inside artery walls.
Heart
A muscular organ roughly the size of a fist, located in the thoracic cavity. Functions as a double pump to circulate blood through pulmonary and systemic circuits.
Atrium (pl. Atria)
The two upper chambers of the heart (left and right). They receive blood returning to the heart from veins and pass it to the ventricles below.
Ventricle
The two lower, thick-walled chambers of the heart (left and right). They pump blood out of the heart — the left ventricle to the body, the right ventricle to the lungs.
Artery
A blood vessel with thick, elastic, muscular walls that carries blood at high pressure away from the heart. Most arteries carry oxygenated blood, except the pulmonary arteries.
Vein
A blood vessel with thinner walls and valves that carries blood at low pressure towards the heart. Most veins carry deoxygenated blood, except pulmonary veins.
Capillary
The smallest and thinnest blood vessel, only one cell thick (endothelium). Forms networks (capillary beds) in all tissues; the site of exchange of oxygen, CO₂, glucose, and waste products between blood and cells.
Haemoglobin
An iron-containing protein found inside red blood cells. Binds reversibly to oxygen (forming oxyhaemoglobin in the lungs) and releases it in oxygen-depleted tissues. Gives blood its red colour.
Plasma
The pale yellow liquid portion of blood (about 55% of blood volume). Transports blood cells, dissolved nutrients (glucose, amino acids), hormones, antibodies, CO₂, urea, and heat.
Cardiac Output (CO)
The volume of blood pumped by each ventricle per minute. CO = Heart Rate × Stroke Volume. Increases significantly during exercise to meet increased oxygen demands.
Pulmonary Circulation
The circuit carrying deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs (for gas exchange) → pulmonary veins → left atrium. Also called the lesser circulation.
Systemic Circulation
The larger circuit carrying oxygenated blood from the left ventricle → aorta → all body organs and tissues → vena cava → right atrium. Supplies oxygen and nutrients to every cell.
Aorta
The largest artery in the body, arising from the left ventricle. Distributes oxygenated blood to all systemic arteries. Has the highest blood pressure in the body.
Vena Cava
The two largest veins returning deoxygenated blood to the right atrium: the superior vena cava (from head/arms) and inferior vena cava (from lower body).
Hypertension
A chronic condition of persistently elevated blood pressure (≥140/90 mmHg). A major risk factor for stroke, coronary heart disease, and kidney disease. Often linked to diet, inactivity, smoking, and stress.
Atherosclerosis
The progressive narrowing of arteries due to build-up of fatty plaques (containing cholesterol) inside arterial walls. Reduces blood flow and can cause heart attack or stroke.
Double Circulation
The system in mammals where blood passes through the heart twice per complete circuit — once through the pulmonary circuit and once through the systemic circuit. Maintains high pressure for efficient oxygen delivery.
